              What this certificate is for — and how the nodes talk.

            

              The certificate below is served by nginx on two ports from the same vhost:
              :443 (public web access — your browser) and :8443 (inter‑node
              health probes between the primary and standby). It does not carry the replication data.
              Node‑to‑node data rides two other channels: Postgres streaming replication on
              :5432 (the standby's live database — hostssl + mutual
              internal‑CA, TLS 1.3, enforced; tuned in the card below) and rsync over SSH
              for the data/ file tree. The full port/channel map and where each sync runs is on the
              High Availability page.

Add certificate authorities

        

          Two slots so a complete path goes in at once: the Root CA is the trust
          anchor, the Intermediate CA is usually what actually signed your
          appliances. Fill either, or both — a chain that stops at an intermediate whose issuer is
          missing imports cleanly and then fails every handshake with unable to get issuer
          certificate, a message that points at the device instead of at the gap.
          
The label is only a hint. Every certificate is
          classified from its own contents (subject == issuer means root), so one
          dropped in the wrong box is still filed correctly, and each box also accepts several
          certificates concatenated. Only CA certificates are accepted: OpenSSL cannot anchor a
          chain on a device's own self-signed leaf, so importing one would look like it worked and
          then fail every handshake.
        

        

          

            
            
            
With more than one certificate the role is appended —
              Acme CA (root), Acme CA (intermediate).
